# Study Sprout — synthetic customer discovery

**All dialogue below is invented for planning. No people were interviewed. These scenarios do not validate demand, establish willingness to pay, or support claims about how common a problem is. Do not submit them as customer quotes or aggregate them into interview statistics.**

## Questions we are trying to answer

- Does choosing the first study task cause enough recurring difficulty to justify a separate product?
- Is the parent the likely buyer, and will the student use the product without repeated prompting?
- Can the family provide useful English-language text notes with little effort?
- Does the confidence-to-review-to-plan loop improve the workflow beyond a checklist or quiz alone?
- Can a tutoring provider or parent community actually recruit appropriate families?

## Scenario A: parent who repeatedly helps with planning — simulated

**Interviewer:** Walk me through the last evening your child prepared for a test.

**Invented parent response:** They had the notes open but asked which part to do first. I helped make a list, then we changed it when another assignment took longer.

**Interviewer:** What have you already tried?

**Invented parent response:** A paper checklist. It works when I help write it. We have not tried a dedicated study app.

**Interviewer:** What would make this app unnecessary?

**Invented parent response:** If I still have to organize the notes and explain every question, it could be more work than the checklist.

**Hypothesis to test:** Parent planning effort is the pain. Observe setup effort and count reminders; interest alone would not establish purchase intent.

## Scenario B: student who resists another tool — simulated

**Interviewer:** How did you choose what to study most recently?

**Invented student response:** I started at the top of the notes. Sometimes I keep rereading the easy section because I know it already.

**Interviewer:** What would stop you using a study plan?

**Invented student response:** Typing everything again or getting a long list that I cannot finish. I do not want another login.

**Interviewer:** What would you need to understand about a review recommendation?

**Invented student response:** Why that topic is next, and how much I need to do today.

**Hypothesis to test:** One visible next task and an explained recommendation are useful. Test uncoached first use; do not assume the student will return because the parent likes it.

## Scenario C: satisfied parent using existing tools — simulated counterexample

**Interviewer:** Tell me how your child prepared for the last test.

**Invented parent response:** They used the teacher's study guide and a calendar. I did not need to intervene.

**Interviewer:** What was difficult about that process?

**Invented parent response:** Understanding one topic, rather than making a schedule.

**Interviewer:** Why might you choose another product?

**Invented parent response:** I would need a reason beyond quizzes and reminders. Our current process is free and familiar.

**Hypothesis to test:** Not all middle-school families are suitable customers. Exclude already-independent families from the initial problem segment; do not equate the enrollment population with demand.

## Scenario D: tutoring provider evaluating a referral — simulated

**Interviewer:** What happens between tutoring sessions?

**Invented tutor response:** Students get practice work, but I cannot tell whether the difficulty is starting it or understanding it.

**Interviewer:** What would you need before recommending a new tool?

**Invented tutor response:** Questions that match the material, a short explanation parents can understand, and a clear account of what data it saves.

**Interviewer:** Would a referral discussion establish a distribution partnership?

**Invented tutor response:** No. We would need to review the product and agree how invitations work.

**Hypothesis to test:** A tutor may be a referral channel rather than the first buyer. Request a concrete invitation commitment and content review before claiming a reachable recruitment pool.

## Scenario E: family with worksheets rather than text notes — simulated counterexample

**Interviewer:** What materials did your child study last time?

**Invented parent response:** Handwritten worksheets, diagrams, and a textbook. There was no text file to paste.

**Interviewer:** What would using this version require?

**Invented parent response:** Someone would need to transcribe the material. That could take longer than helping directly.

**Hypothesis to test:** Text input is a meaningful adoption boundary. Recruit families with suitable notes for the first pilot and record excluded families. Investigate import improvements only if real discovery shows this is the main blocker.

## Product decisions to test, not validated findings

| Assumption raised by the simulation | Smallest next step | Evidence against the idea |
|---|---|---|
| Parents want less repeated planning help | Observe one subject and test with the current app | Setup creates more parent work than the usual checklist |
| Students need a specific next action | Observe an uncoached first session | Students still need an adult to choose or explain the task |
| Confidence-aware review is useful | Ask students to explain a recommendation after a quiz | They cannot understand it or prefer the original schedule |
| Text notes are readily available in the initial segment | Inspect the materials used for the last test, with permission | Most eligible families need substantial transcription |
| A community can recruit a pilot | Obtain permission and count actual invitations and signups | No organizer commits to inviting families |
| Parents may pay after repeated value | Later offer an explicitly priced pilot | Stated interest does not turn into actual paid opt-ins |

## Real validation plan

1. Interview 10 parents and, with parent permission and student assent, their children. Ask about the most recent test before showing the product. Ask what happened, how often it happens, what they tried, and what the consequence was. Record disconfirming examples as well as supportive ones. Do not feed simulated answers to participants.
2. Seek one tutoring provider or parent-community organizer to invite 20 eligible families. Record invitations, signups, eligibility, and dropouts. No partner or participants are currently secured.
3. Measure three usual-workflow evenings and three app evenings per family, using comparable tasks where possible. Define planning time as opening materials to choosing the first specific study task. Log parent reminders and first-use failures. Use consented research logs rather than adding student analytics.
4. Compute each family's median baseline and app planning times, then the percentage change for families with a positive baseline. Report the median paired percentage change, paired sample count, missing observations, and zero baselines separately. **Proposed target: at least 30% lower median planning time.** A small before/after pilot is exploratory and cannot establish causation or improved learning.
5. Over two weeks, record voluntary return use. **Proposed threshold: at least 12 of 20 enrolled students return on three distinct study days.** Keep all enrolled students in that denominator, including dropouts; disclose missing logs.
6. If the workflow is useful, test a stated price through actual paid pilot opt-ins. Track referrals separately from buyers and revenue. Do not infer willingness to pay from a synthetic response, a compliment, or a test pass.
