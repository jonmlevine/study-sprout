01 / DEMAND REALITY & PROBLEM SEVERITY 25%

The hard part is deciding what to study next.

A specific pain

A student has an exam in seven days, several pages of notes, and 30 minutes a day. Reading everything again does not reveal which ideas need attention. Planning, checking recall, and rebuilding the schedule are separate jobs.

Our problem hypothesis: this decision work leads to avoidable review of familiar material and delayed attention to misconceptions. Its frequency and severity in our target group still need interviews.

Why this is worth investigating

95% of respondents in HEPI / Kortext’s 2026 survey reported using AI in at least one way. The survey covered 1,054 full-time UK undergraduates, polled in December 2025. This supports interest in the category, not demand for Study Sprout specifically. Read the survey, pp. 4, 9–10 ↗

A review of learning techniques rated practice testing and distributed practice highly. It motivates our design, but does not validate our algorithm or establish improved grades. APS research summary ↗

02 / TARGET CUSTOMER & MARKET SCOPE 20%

Start with one student and one exam.

INITIAL USER

First-year college STEM students

Students with English-language lecture notes, an upcoming exam, and a limited daily study window. The prototype accepts text; it does not yet support PDF extraction or every subject format.

INITIAL CHANNEL

Campus tutoring centers

Recruit volunteers through one learning-support program and course study groups. Tutoring-center distribution and purchasing interest are hypotheses to test, not current partnerships.

WHY COME BACK?

A plan that changes with evidence

The app connects source notes, recall, confidence, and calendar capacity. A confident mistake can become the next review session, with its source shown and a reason attached.

Market scope is intentionally narrow.

First milestone: 20 consenting student volunteers over two weeks. An illustrative expansion scenario is 5 centers × 200 learners = 1,000 seats; it is a planning assumption, not a measured market, forecast, or commitment.

03 / SOLUTION FIT & PRODUCT DESIGN 20%

From “where do I start?” to “here’s my next step.”

BEFORE · PROBLEM HYPOTHESIS
  1. Collect notes in one place.
  2. Guess which topics need attention.
  3. Manually create a study calendar.
  4. Miss a day and rebuild it.
→
AFTER · WORKING PROTOTYPE
  1. Paste notes, set an exam date and daily budget.
  2. Answer questions and report confidence.
  3. Read evidence for any missed answer.
  4. Preview a revised plan that fits available time.

The key action made easier: choosing and scheduling the next useful study session. Completion tracks activity; quiz accuracy is a recall signal, not an exam-readiness score.

Catch confident mistakes

A wrong answer marked “Very sure” gets higher priority than a correct answer marked “I’m guessing.” Every recommendation has an explanation.

Recover without overload

Rebalance unfinished work from today. Review suggestions use the latest quiz evidence; previews show daily totals and block changes that would lose work.

Keep the source visible

Questions include source excerpts. OCI responses are schema-checked and their quotes are checked against the notes. Semantic correctness still needs human review.

Budgeted scheduler

After each completed quiz, a deterministic browser-side coach ranks review needs and previews a new schedule. AI is used for content understanding and question generation when connected; dates, capacity limits, confidence rules, and saved progress remain explicit application logic. No autonomous cloud actions occur.

Implementation and limits

Dependency-free HTML/CSS/JavaScript; Node HTTP server; browser localStorage; signed OCI GENERIC chat REST adapter. API keys stay on the server. Content is escaped before display, public files are allowlisted, request sizes and generation concurrency are bounded. No cloud database, accounts, PDF/OCR, or cross-device sync. The Dockerfile is included; cloud deployment is still pending.

New: the Learning lab

Explain-back: write from memory, reveal exact source excerpts, and flag missing or uncertain points. Self-identified gaps update the review queue without being counted as quiz accuracy.

Exam recovery: mark unavailable days and change your budget. Inspect scheduled work, overflow, and completed records before applying.

Learning evidence: compare first and latest answers on the same questions and inspect repeated recall after 48 hours. These are observed practice results, not a controlled learning study.

The three-minute judge walkthrough

  1. 0:00 — Show the student. Explain the notes, exam date, and daily study budget. State the actual generation mode.
  2. 0:25 — Explain an idea. Open Learning lab, choose a topic and write from memory. Reveal the source points, mark one missing, and save the self-check.
  3. 1:15 — Show a useful consequence. Inspect the new topic priority. In Exam recovery, mark a day off and reduce the budget to reveal overflow. Restore enough time, inspect the sessions, and apply.
  4. 2:00 — Measure, do not assume. Refresh to show persistence. Open Learning progress or Time benefit and explain what the actual records measure. Empty results mean evidence has not yet been collected.
  5. 2:30 — Show demand and execution evidence. Open the pilot console for real observations, run live verification, and state the remaining OCI and deployment work.

Business model hypothesis

Offer a free individual workflow and test a campus-supported cohort license. The proposed buyer is a tutoring or academic-support program. Pricing, willingness to pay, support costs, and OCI cost per active learner remain unvalidated.

What would become harder to copy?

The current prototype is easy to copy. A future advantage could come from consented longitudinal outcome data, tutor-validated question quality, and trusted campus distribution. None of those assets exists yet.

What the next version must prove

  • Usability target: median time to a useful next step under two minutes, measured without coaching.
  • Repeat-use target: at least 12 of 20 pilot students return on three separate study days.
  • Learning test: compare 48-hour recall on unseen questions against the students’ usual approach, with a controlled protocol and no grade-improvement claim from a small pilot.
  • Commercial test: interview five program leads and measure willingness to pilot and pay.
  • Technical gate: verify real OCI quality, latency, and cost; publish a secured HTTPS demo.
The ask:

One learning-support partner, 20 student volunteers, and OCI sandbox access to test whether this study loop creates value people return for. These are proposed next steps, not existing traction.
